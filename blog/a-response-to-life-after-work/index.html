The value of human work: a response to "Life after work"

The St. Vitus Cathedral in Prague took nearly 600 years to construct. A testament to the human spirit, collaboration, and architecture, its value lies in the countless hours and resources spent on its construction. The fact that cathedrals like this were built in the name of faith demonstrates the value of human work.

July 13, 2026

How will full automation of value-creating activities in the economy (i.e. work) make “most people vastly better off”? The authors present a convincing and optimistic view of the future resulting from the full automation of human work with artificial intelligence (AI). They argue that AI, absent from biological limitations, can be trained to do nearly any work activity that a human can with a great potential to scale in size, historically growing proportionally to gross domestic product (GDP). Generally, I find it difficult to disagree with the benefits that this optimistic vision offers to all, and I find it equally difficult to ignore the pessimistic view I hold of federal governments and large corporations in a capitalist economy.

Work is fundamental to the human experience

We sit at a strange time in history: I feel that we have collectively reached an apex of “make-work” in society, with many people simply going through the motions of working just to pay the bills. Stagnation in Canada’s economy exemplifies this feeling of treading water, and I suspect that the Canadian government does not (currently) mind the stagnant production, since they are happy to collect income tax without regard to the output it is paying for. The strange part comes with the beast that is automation waiting to pounce. It feels like a cloud looming over us all, one that we can’t ignore but can’t openly discuss either: it invokes some form of fear or existential dread that almost no one appears to have the capacity to face, perhaps because full-time work has been so deeply entrenched in daily life. I make this claim - that most people have internalised the knowledge of their inevitable job loss, knowing that the work they do has little real value or impact - with no evidence. I will thus be the first to admit that I could be wrong, I may be part of a minority that feel like this, I may have a skewed worldview due to my limited experience (both in work and number of years lived), or I may be projecting my own opinions on others. This aside, the avoidance with which the labour force faces the future signals a pain point. Not only because wages (a primary source of income) will be lost with little direction on how this loss might be picked up elsewhere, but also because man wants to work.

No different than other intelligent mammals, humans enjoy a satisfaction achieved with observing tangible, positive, and immediate effects from work performed. We want to act on the world, and hard work is not something that ought to be discarded in a matter of years for the sake of “liberation” from work. The benefits and morality associated with outlawing child labour are obvious, and the authors draw a convincing parallel to the current situation with automated work, but with one issue: the subjects today are adults, not children. I think that the same benefits will not be observed with a mass reduction in adult labour as were observed after the reduction of child labour; I am not suggesting that adults in the labour force should all continue to be locked to a job from which they gain no joy or satisfaction, but I believe that many people will become apathetic with no work to do.

An obvious rebuttal is that adults have the freedom to choose how they want to spend their time and will/should choose to spend it being productive by their own will. For those motivated extrinsically, there is value in a structured day with clear responsibilities, though this same structure can hinder many who are intrinsically motivated. The freedom resulting from full automation will be liberating to many and a trial of independence to others. I make these statements - perhaps because I have been a student for as long as I can remember - as one who appreciates direction and assigned tasks. Not everyone is perfectly self-guided and fully comfortable leaving this structure behind. To be clear, I am not assuming that the authors are discounting the value of hard work, in fact I am confident that they embrace it (this is clear from the work done to build and operate Mechanize). Regardless, the authors state that AI will “liberate people from work”, and I think that a little more care should be used when dealing with the value of work because of its deep embedding in our daily lives, cultures, history, and instincts.

Goverrnments cannot replace wages as fast as AI will appropriate them

Fundamentally, I agree with the thesis that wages, a primary source of income in most (if not all) major economies today, can be replaced with government transfers and investment income; in fact I believe that if full automation is to be implemented, replacement of income via redistribution of the excess wealth generated is the only moral and economically feasible course. The authors claim that wealthy people would not attempt (or would not be successful if attempted) a prevention of wealth redistribution. While I am biased to believe the opposite (an opinion that I think is not wildly unpopular), I will forgo it and instead focus on the slow-moving and often incompetent nature of federal governments. Despite the fact that an expansion of economic prosperity will follow with an increase in government financial support, I see a serious lag here that cannot be ignored: the rate at which AI replaces human work will quickly surpass the rate at which the government replaces those lost wages. Full automation in its final stages with an appropriate implementation of universal basic income (UBI) should result in overall positive impacts on the lives of the global population, freeing time and all but eradicating household/individual financial concerns. However I cannot see a world where governments act swiftly and competently to ensure the financial stability of their citizens, even in the authors’ scenario of taxes earned from a “ten-fold increase” in GDP.

The authors describe a very bright future where no one must work and everyone earns enough to live comfortably by the grace of the government and the elite few that control the AI means of production (we have already seen a historic transfer of wealth toward stakeholders and owners in hardware, energy, software, distribution, etc. since the US stock market AI boom). I have no reason not to support the vision and hope to see it come true; I believe that in its final stage, the benefits of full automation with a competent delivery of UBI will outweigh any drawbacks. I admit that earning and spending income to satisfy basic needs is more important on a societal level, thus I am willing to set aside my argument that humans broadly appreciate hard work. Still, I think that the AI sector and the federal government represent two extremes of action pace (I admit that it is an apples to oranges comparison, but these are the two players we have been given to work with). AI chatbots, fueled by fierce competition globally, were the most quickly developed and immediately adopted (by society) technology of which I am aware. Governments, usually larger than firms and not fueled by competition, are known for bureaucracy and a lack of capacity for change management. The inherently smaller and more agile sector of AI will move more quickly to appropriate wages than governing bodies will move to distribute a sufficient replacement of income. I do believe that there is a graceful way to proceed: the rates at which the government and AI sector act should approach one another (meeting somewhere in between, not necessary the “middle”). The full replacement of human workers need not be realized as soon as possible, and governments must act with more haste than expected to ensure the financial stability of its citizens and to get ahead of any foul play that the AI elite may attempt.

Afterword

As is the case with any new technology, a rejection upon initial release is expected, and the reception of AI was no different. There was some presence of internet users that were quick to reject outright anything to do with generative AI: anything created with it, inspired by it, or supporting it. Several years after the full release of AI tools, it has become clear that this segment of users is not budging on the issue (perhaps individuals will switch sides, but the overall population of the segment is not approaching zero). Moreover, the issue of an internet that drowns human content out with AI-generated content is a real and increasingly relevant concern that should be dealt with carefully.

AI-generated content has revealed the true value that many people assign to human creation; the value of lots of art and creation is derivative of the time, effort, and overall energy put into it. AI exists almost as an antithetical servant of this philosophy, where the goal is to increase productive output by cutting out all human effort except for the prompt given (AI can more or less run on its own now with humans merely monitoring progress and keeping the electricity running). This is nothing new, really: much of medieval, renaissance, and baroque fine art were testaments to religious devotion. Cathedrals are the most obvious example, some of which took centuries to complete. These pieces were valuable because of the immense devotion of time and effort to their creation.

I cannot understand the authors’ logic in the following statement, perhaps due to my own short-sightedness or inexperience.

Today, most families own some assets like retirement portfolios and real estate, even though these assets provide only a small portion of their current income. Since full automation will make the world dramatically richer, owning even small amounts of capital will likely be sufficient to secure a comfortable retirement. As a result, most people will probably benefit enormously from full automation.

Barnett, Besiroglu, Erdil (2025)
From ownership of some valuable asset (a generous assumption on its own) and full automation making the world richer, how does it follow that such capital secures a comfortable retirement? Is it because the abundance of goods will drive consumer prices down?
